When Excessive Process Defeats Perfection

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

Every control you add costs something. An executive's view on the point where process stops protecting the work and starts preventing it.

Every process begins with a good intention. A mistake was made, so a check was added. A customer was hurt, so an approval was added. An auditor asked a hard question, so a form was born.

Nobody sets out to build a bureaucracy. Bureaucracy is what accumulates — one reasonable decision at a time — until the day the organization realizes it spends more energy serving its processes than serving its customers.

And here's the uncomfortable part: the processes built in the name of perfection are usually the ones that defeat it.

The math nobody does

Every control has a price, and it's paid in full, every time, forever: the minutes of every person who touches it, multiplied by every transaction, indefinitely. The defect it prevents has a price too — usually a probability multiplied by a cost, and often a small one.

Rational process design compares the two numbers. Excessive process never does the math. It treats every risk as if it were catastrophic and every precaution as if it were free.

The result is a system tuned to catch the last 1% of errors by taxing the other 99% of the work. Eventually the tax exceeds the loss it was preventing — and nobody notices, because the cost is spread across everyone's calendar in ten-minute increments, while the benefit shows up in a report nobody reads.

Process theater

Past a certain point, controls stop controlling anything. They become theater.

It's the approval chain where the fourth approver has never once rejected anything — he's a signature, not a check. The mandatory field nobody downstream ever uses, filled in because the form refuses to proceed without it. The stack of attestations clicked through in nine seconds, which don't create accountability — they create evidence that accountability was simulated.

Theater is worse than no process at all, because it produces the feeling of control without the substance. Leadership believes the risk is managed. It isn't. It's documented.

The human cost is the real cost

Excessive process is a message, and your people hear it clearly: we don't trust you.

Craftsmen — the experienced people you can least afford to lose — feel it first and worst. They know which steps protect the work and which ones protect the organization from embarrassment, and they resent being taxed for the second kind. They also have options. The people who stay and thrive in a heavy-process culture tend to be the ones most comfortable with process — which is not the same thing as being best at the work.

Meanwhile, the process quietly becomes the product. Teams optimize for passing the audit, closing the form, clearing the queue. The customer, whose name appears nowhere in the workflow, waits.

The quality view: control by risk, not by fear

I spent years in quality at Boeing, in an industry where some failures are genuinely unforgivable. Aviation taught me the opposite of what most people assume: the organizations with the strongest safety records are not the ones with the most process. They're the ones that know exactly which controls are sacred.

Where failure is catastrophic, you control absolutely — redundancy, independence, no shortcuts, no exceptions. Where failure is cheap and reversible, you move fast and fix forward. The sin isn't having process. The sin is having one speed of process: treating a typo like a turbine failure — and, by diluting attention across a thousand trivial controls, quietly treating the turbine failure like a typo.

Attention is the scarcest resource in any control system. Spend it everywhere, and it's effective nowhere.

Five questions that cut process back to size

Before any step earns its place — and periodically after — ask:

  • What defect does this step actually prevent? If nobody can name it, the step is decoration.
  • What does that defect cost, and how often does it happen? Price the risk honestly before pricing the cure.
  • What does this step cost — every time, forever, across everyone who touches it? That's the number nobody calculates and everybody pays.
  • Who reads this, and what decision changes because of it? Information nobody acts on isn't oversight. It's litter.
  • What would we do with the time this step consumes? Process competes with the work itself for the same hours. Make it win that competition on merit.

Discipline is not volume

Real discipline is a short list of controls that matter, enforced without exception. Bureaucracy is a long list enforced without thought. The first produces excellence. The second produces compliance — and compliance mistaken for quality is how organizations become perfectly documented and quietly mediocre.

My whole approach to process work comes down to finding where work waits and removing the wait. Excessive process is wait, manufactured deliberately and defended as virtue.

The goal was never a perfect process. The goal is a perfect outcome for the customer — and there's a difference. Process is the scaffolding.

Nobody buys the scaffolding.
