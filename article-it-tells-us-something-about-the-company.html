It Tells Us Something About the Company

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 14, 2026

This morning, applying for a single job took four verification codes, two screenshots, and twenty-five minutes — not because anything was broken, but because everything was working exactly as designed.

I run my job search the way I've run every operation in my career: with staff. My staff happens to be an AI agent — authorized by me, acting in my name, with my résumé, my answers, and my instructions. This morning that agent was applying to a job I had chosen, on a major job board, and it hit a wall that had nothing to do with my qualifications.

The wall was a login. Not a password — passwords are considered too convenient now. A six-digit code, emailed to me, presented in six separate little boxes on the screen. The boxes are engineered so that software cannot fill them in. Twice the secure, system-approved method of entering the code failed — not because the code was wrong, but because the field is built to reject anything that isn't human fingers on a keyboard. In the end, I was photographing codes off my own phone and relaying them by hand, a human courier running messages between two systems that are perfectly capable of talking to each other but have been deliberately forbidden to.

Nothing about that was an accident. And that's the subject of this article.

The technique has a purpose, and the purpose isn't you

The technique is called friction by design: human-intervention gates placed between you and the machine. Email codes instead of credentials. Segmented code boxes that defeat form-filling. "Prove you're human" checkpoints scattered through a process like tollbooths. The stated purpose is security and fraud prevention, and there is real fraud in the world — nobody serious disputes that.

But let's be precise about who these gates actually stop.

They do not stop the fraud operations. Industrial-scale abuse employs human beings by the thousand to solve captchas and type codes for pennies a click. A gate that costs a criminal operation a fraction of a cent costs it nothing.

They do not stop the determined scraper or the bot farm. Those rotate devices, residential proxies, and human farms faster than the gates evolve.

Who they stop — reliably, every single time — is the legitimate individual using an authorized agent. The executive who has delegated the mechanical work so his hours go to judgment. The person with a disability whose assistive technology is, by definition, software acting on their behalf. The older worker whose "agent" is a son or daughter helping from three states away. The gate cannot tell the difference between a botnet and a man's own assistant. It doesn't try to. It treats every delegation as an attack.

Friction isn't eliminated. It's exported.

Here's the accounting nobody publishes. A company that installs these gates saves itself real money: fewer fake applications, less support burden, cleaner data. That savings appears on its ledger.

The cost doesn't disappear. It moves — onto the individual's side of the ledger, paid in the only currency the individual can't print: time. Twenty-five minutes of mine this morning, multiplied by every applicant, every customer, every citizen trying to renew, file, apply, or access. The company has not made its process efficient. It has made its process efficient for itself, and sent the invoice to people who were never asked.

I spent thirty years in process optimization. My rule is simple: find where work waits and remove the wait. These gates are the opposite discipline — find where the company's work waits, and move the wait onto the customer. It is a legitimate strategy. But let's call it by its name: it is the company choosing itself over the individual, one six-digit code at a time.

The front door is the first honest page of the culture deck

Every company publishes values. Most of them say something about people. I've learned to read a company's culture somewhere more reliable than its careers page: its front door.

How an institution treats a person who has no leverage over it yet — an applicant, a customer at a login screen, a citizen at a portal — is how that institution treats people. The application process is a candidate's first working sample of the company's management philosophy. A process that opens with "we do not trust you, we will not accommodate how you work, and your time is worth nothing to us" has told the candidate something true about what life is like on the inside of that wall. The interview will be friendlier. It will not be more honest.

And this cuts the other way, too. The companies in my search that made it easy to be evaluated on my merits — standard credentials, a form that respects the person filling it, a human being reachable when the machine reached its limit — told me something about themselves as well. I remember which ones they are. So does every candidate.

The fork in the road is already here

The age of personal agents is not coming; it arrived, and I am one of its early ordinary users — not a technologist playing with a toy, but a 60-year-old executive who found that an authorized machine does the mechanical work better than he does, and keeps the human work for himself. There will be millions of us, and soon after, it will simply be how everyone works.

Companies now choose between two designs. They can verify authorization — prove that the agent acts for a real person who stands behind it, and then let the person work the way people now work. Or they can keep building tollbooths and demand the human's personal minutes as the price of admission.

Both designs function. Only one of them respects the person on the other side of the screen.

Closing exhibit: the door with no room behind it

I promised you a postscript, and the same morning delivered it.

The code saga above was the price of signing in to apply for a vice president role at a data-center company — a role my research had verified six ways. Once my agent finally got through the gate (with me relaying codes from my phone like a wartime courier), the sign-in worked. And then we discovered the rest of the story: the application form does not exist. The job is genuinely open — the company's own recruiting system confirms it, requisition number and all — but the company's careers page runs on a different feed that doesn't list the role at all, and every official link loops back to that same page. Four verification codes, twenty-five minutes, a human being conscripted as a data cable — to arrive at a door with no room behind it.

Sit with the priorities that reveals. The company had invested in the machinery of keeping people out: the codes, the segmented boxes, the human checks, all maintained and working. The machinery of letting the right person in — a live posting connected to a working form — was broken, and apparently nobody's job was to notice. Security theater at the gate; an empty building behind it.

That is the part of this story I can't stop thinking about, because it generalizes. An organization gets the applicant experience it maintains. If the gates are polished and the welcome is broken, the organization has told you — precisely, in the only language that can't be spun — what it values.

So when a company reaches for these tools, it should do so knowingly. Installing a human-intervention gate is not an IT decision. It is a decision about the relative worth of the company's convenience and the individual's time — a decision to choose the company over the person, made quietly, at scale, every day.

It tells us something about the company.

Candidates are allowed to listen.
