Should Companies Choose People Over AI?

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

It's the wrong question — and answering it wrong, in either direction, loses. An executive's view.

It's the debate everywhere now: should companies choose people over AI?

I understand the impulse behind the question. It comes from a decent place — loyalty to the humans who built the company. But as posed, it's a trap, because it accepts a binary that doesn't exist in any real operation. No functioning executive chooses "people" or "AI" the way you choose a vendor. You choose, function by function and decision by decision, where judgment lives and where throughput lives.

Companies that answer the binary wrong in the AI direction will hollow themselves out. Companies that answer it wrong in the people direction will price themselves out of their markets. Both will call it principle. Both will be wrong.

Here's the framework I actually use.

Choose people where trust is the product

Some of what a company sells is throughput. Much of the best of it is trust. Customers renew because a person knows them, because someone answers for outcomes, because in the worst week of the year a human being picked up the phone and owned the problem.

Where trust is the product, people aren't a cost to optimize — they are the offering. Automate the work around them, absolutely. Automate them, and you've cut the product and kept the packaging.

Choose people where judgment decides

AI is superb inside the lines: known inputs, defined rules, measurable outputs. Business, however, keeps happening outside the lines — the ambiguous call, the incomplete data, the decision that's really about values disguised as a decision about numbers.

Judgment under ambiguity is the scarcest executive resource, and it does not come from a model. It comes from people who have been wrong before and remember what it cost. Choose people there, every time, and don't apologize for the salary line. It's the cheapest insurance in the building.

Choose people where accountability needs a name

An AI cannot be accountable. It cannot be fired, trusted, or believed — only used. Every consequential decision in a serious company has a human name on it, or it has no owner, and ownerless decisions are how companies end up in front of congressional committees explaining that the system did it.

If a choice can ruin a customer, a career, or the company, a person makes it and a person answers for it. That is not nostalgia. That is governance.

And choose AI — without guilt — where the work is machine work

Now the other direction, stated just as plainly: keeping humans on rules-based, high-volume, repetitive work is not "choosing people." It's choosing to burn your people out on tasks a machine does better, while your competitor's people do the interesting work. Loyalty that takes the form of protecting obsolete tasks isn't loyalty. It's slow-motion layoffs with better PR.

Choosing people was never supposed to mean freezing every job in its 2019 shape. Jobs change. They always have. The obligation isn't to preserve the task list — it's to carry the people forward into the new one.

The real choice: who gets the dividend?

Strip the debate down and the actual question is this: when AI creates a gain — hours saved, cost removed, capacity created — who receives it?

Companies that route the entire dividend to headcount reduction will get exactly one cycle of gains, because their surviving employees will spend the second cycle quietly resisting, routing around, and sandbagging the tools. People are not stupid. If every efficiency they create deletes a colleague, they will stop creating efficiencies. You'll have chosen AI, and you'll get neither AI's benefits nor your people's best.

Companies that share the dividend — time back, better work, retraining into the judgment roles, a visible path from "the tool took my task" to "the tool promoted my job" — become adoption machines. Their people surface the use cases no consultant would find, because it's finally in their interest to.

Choosing people, it turns out, is not the soft option. It's the adoption strategy.

The decision rule

For any function, ask one question: is the value in the throughput, or in the trust?

Throughput — automate it, aggressively and without apology. Trust — staff it with your best people, and arm those people with every tool that exists. Most roles are a bundle of both, which is why the real executive work is unbundling: strip the machine work out of human jobs, and strip the human work out of machine processes, until each side is doing what it's actually for.

My own pillars, in order, have always been people first and technical excellence. Note that they're both on the list. The order is the strategy — and the second pillar is how you afford the first.

The answer

Should companies choose people over AI? Companies should choose people — and then put the most powerful tool in history in their hands.

That's not a compromise between the two answers. It is the answer. The firms that win the next decade won't be the ones that picked a side in a fake war. They'll be the ones whose people got stronger — because leadership understood that AI was never the alternative to people.

It was always the argument for investing in them.
