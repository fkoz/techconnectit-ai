When To Choose AI — And When To Not

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

A field guide for executives who are tired of the hype in both directions.

Every executive I know is being pressured from two sides at once. On one side: adopt AI everywhere, immediately, or be left behind. On the other: cautionary tales, failed pilots and a workforce quietly terrified of being automated out of a livelihood.

Both pressures are useless for the decision that actually matters, which is never "AI or no AI." It's the hundred specific decisions underneath: this task, this team, this customer touchpoint — machine or human?

I've spent weeks running a live operation with an AI agent doing real work at real scale. Here's the framework that fell out of it.

Choose AI When The Work Is Machine-Shaped

AI earns its keep when the task has a clear profile:

Volume and repetition. Thousands of similar transactions, documents, applications or checks. Machines don't get bored, and boredom is where human error breeds.

Rules that can be stated. If you can write down what "correct" means, a machine can apply it tirelessly — screening, matching, validating, tracking, scheduling, monitoring.

Errors that are cheap and reversible. A draft that gets edited, a flag that gets reviewed, a recommendation a human can override. When being wrong costs a correction, automate freely.

Clean data and clear inputs. AI on top of good data is leverage. AI on top of garbage is garbage at scale — faster, more confident and harder to catch.

Speed that humans structurally can't match. Watching a hundred boards, prices or signals around the clock. Nobody should pay a person to stare at a screen waiting for a change.

In my own operation, this is exactly the work the machine took: 150-plus job applications researched, verified and submitted; daily scans; codes and deadlines tracked to the minute. It is superb at it. I will never do that work by hand again, and neither should your teams.

Do Not Choose AI When The Work Is Human-Shaped

And here is the other profile — the one the sales decks skip:

Judgment under ambiguity. When the data is incomplete, the situation is novel and the decision turns on values, context and experience — that's a person. Models interpolate the past. Leaders have to judge a future that doesn't resemble it.

Trust is the product. The negotiation, the apology, the save, the relationship. Customers can tell when they're being handled by a system, and the message received is what you're actually worth to the company. Where trust is what's being sold, a human delivers it.

Accountability must have a name. If the decision can ruin a customer, a career or the company, a person makes it and a person answers for it. "The system decided" is not accountability; it's an alibi.

Errors are irreversible or catastrophic. I spent years in quality and safety in aviation. Where failure is unforgivable, control stays human, redundant and absolute — no efficiency argument survives contact with that standard. Every industry has its version of the flight-critical function. Know yours.

The relationship IS the work. Mentoring. Leading a team through a failure. Sitting with someone in the worst week of their year. Automating these doesn't make them efficient. It makes them absent.

The Middle Is Where The Money Is

Between the two profiles sits the pattern that actually wins: human-in-the-loop, designed deliberately.

Let the AI draft and the human decide. Let the AI flag and the human act. Let the AI carry the routine 95% and route the exceptions — the weird, the angry, the high-stakes, the unprecedented — to people who are fresh for them precisely because the machine took the routine off their plates.

Notice what that does to the human job: it doesn't shrink it. It concentrates it into the parts with the highest value and the deepest job satisfaction. My own days now are direction, judgment and relationships, because everything else has a machine under it. That's not a smaller role. It's the executive role, finally stripped of its clerical disguise.

Five Questions Before You Automate Anything

  1. Can "correct" be written down? If not, a human owns it.
  2. What does an error cost, and can it be undone? Cheap and reversible — automate. Catastrophic or permanent — don't.
  3. Is trust what's being transacted? If yes, staff it with people and arm them with AI behind the scenes.
  4. Is the data clean? If not, fix the data first. Automating on dirty data is just manufacturing errors at industrial speed.
  5. Who is the named human accountable for the outcome? If nobody can answer, you're not ready to automate it — you're ready to abdicate it.

The Bottom Line

Choose AI for throughput. Choose people for trust, judgment and accountability. And choose the hybrid — machine underneath, human on top — for almost everything in between, which is to say, almost everything.

The executives who get this right won't be the ones who adopted the most AI or resisted it the longest. They'll be the ones who learned, decision by decision, which work was machine-shaped and which was human-shaped — and refused to confuse the two.

That discernment is the actual AI strategy. Everything else is procurement.
