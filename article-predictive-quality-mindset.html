Predict It or Pay for It

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

Driving quality with a predictive, proactive mindset — an executive's view.

Most organizations manage quality by looking in the rearview mirror.

Defect counts. Escape reports. Warranty claims. Audit findings. Customer complaints. Every one of those numbers describes something that already went wrong — usually weeks or months ago, and usually at the customer's expense.

That's not quality management. That's archaeology.

Lagging indicators tell you what it cost. Leading indicators tell you what's coming.

A lagging indicator is an outcome: the defect that shipped, the failure in the field, the finding in the audit. By definition, you can't act on it. You can only pay for it and apologize.

A leading indicator is a signal: a process drifting toward its limit before it crosses it, a supplier whose measurements are trending the wrong way, a near-miss that almost became an escape, a gap in training right before a team takes on new work. Leading indicators arrive while you can still do something cheap about them.

So the executive question is simple: which set of numbers runs your quality reviews? If the first slide is always what already failed, you're managing history.

The economics are not subtle

Quality professionals have known this math for generations. An error prevented in design costs dollars. The same error caught in production costs hundreds. Caught by your customer, it costs thousands — plus the one currency you can't buy back: trust.

Every step downstream multiplies the price and shrinks your options. A proactive mindset is, at bottom, an economic decision: spend small money early, on purpose, instead of large money late, by surprise.

In my years as a quality engineer working safety and quality at Boeing, the lesson repeated itself constantly: the expensive problems were almost never surprises. The signals were there — in the trend data, in the near-misses, in the process that had been drifting for weeks. The organizations that got hurt weren't missing data. They were missing the habit of acting on signals before the signals became statistics.

What proactive actually looks like

  • Instrument the process, not just the outcome. If you only measure the finished product, you've chosen to learn about problems at the most expensive possible moment. Measure the process while there's still time to steer it.
  • Trend the drift, not just the failures. A process can be "in spec" and still be heading for the wall. Control limits exist to catch the drift before it becomes a defect. Watch the trend line, not just the red and the green.
  • Treat near-misses as free lessons. A near-miss is a failure that didn't charge you tuition. Organizations that log them, trend them, and act on them are buying tomorrow's quality at a discount.
  • Build the error out of the process. The best inspection is the one you don't need. Error-proof the step — design the work so the wrong action is hard or impossible — and quality stops depending on vigilance.
  • Push quality upstream to your suppliers. Your customer's experience includes your supply chain. Trending supplier data and acting on early signals beats incoming inspection, which is just paying to sort someone else's problems.
  • Review leading indicators in the same rhythm as results. What leadership asks about first is what the organization optimizes. Put the predictive signals at the front of the review, not in the appendix.

The culture piece is the real work

None of this survives contact with a culture that punishes bad news. If the person who surfaces a problem early gets blamed for the problem, your people will stop surfacing problems — and you'll go back to hearing about them from customers.

A proactive quality culture rewards the early flag. The engineer who says "this is drifting" in March is worth more than the task force that explains the failure in October. Leaders set that price, whether they mean to or not. Every reaction to bad news is a policy decision about whether you'll ever get early news again.

And yes — this is where good data earns its keep

Prediction runs on data: timely, trusted, and trended. The analytics and AI tools available today can spot drift patterns across thousands of signals that no human review board would catch. But they only predict well when the underlying data is clean and process owners act on what the signals say. A prediction nobody acts on is just a more sophisticated rearview mirror.

The mindset, in one line

Reactive quality asks: "What went wrong?" Proactive quality asks: "What's about to?"

My whole approach to process work is finding where the problem enters and fixing it there — before it becomes work, cost, or a customer's bad day. Quality is no different. The cheapest defect is the one that never happens, and the leaders who understand that stop paying for quality twice: once to build it wrong, and again to explain it.

Predict it, or pay for it. Those are the options.
