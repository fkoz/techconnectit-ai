Schedule Sprawl

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC

How we cut 67 scheduled tasks a week down to 21 — and saved one full working day's budget every month.

Nobody sets out to build a bloated schedule. It grows the way every system grows: one reasonable request at a time.

Check this job board. Then check it daily. Watch this inbox — and watch it again at night, just in case. Remind me about that. Scan for this. Each addition was small, each one made sense on the day it was added, and nobody ever subtracted anything. By the end of October, my automated schedule was firing 67 recurring runs a week — and on October 30, the bill came due in the most literal way possible: the day's work burned through its entire token budget, tripped the hard stop we had set, and shut the whole schedule down by early evening.

That forced stop turned out to be a gift. It made the sprawl visible. So that night, instead of just turning everything back on, we audited the entire schedule — every job, every cadence, every line of every job's instructions — and rebuilt it. This is what we found, what we changed, and what it saved.

Pass One: The Structure

The first pass ignored what the jobs did and looked only at how they were arranged. Four kinds of waste showed up immediately.

Duplication. Two different job scans were sweeping the same employer boards and reporting the same finds. A nighttime email watch was re-running, almost verbatim, the watch the morning pass had already done. Nine separate job-board checks each started their own session to do work that one shared session could do in sequence. Duplication never announces itself; each job looks reasonable alone. You only see it when you lay the whole schedule side by side.

Cadence creep. Slow-moving job boards — the kind that produce a qualifying opening every few weeks — were being checked as often as fast-moving ones. "Daily" had become the default rather than a decision. Matching cadence to actual velocity (daily where things move, Sundays only where they don't, monthly where a weekly glance was pure habit) cost nothing in coverage.

Collisions and pile-ups. Four monthly jobs all landed on the 1st of the month — on a Sunday that was also the heaviest regular day. Two Sunday jobs fired the same minute while a third, longer job was still running. Nothing was broken, exactly; everything was just stacked.

Dead weight. Six one-off jobs whose dates had passed or whose purpose had been satisfied were still sitting in the list. Worse, the bodies of running jobs carried expired instructions — a "special, this week only" section from two weeks prior, still being loaded and paid for on every run. Schedules don't just accumulate jobs. They accumulate text.

Pass Two: The Value

The second pass asked a harder question of every remaining job: what did you produce lately, and was it worth your runs?

One board check had produced zero qualifying roles, run after run — cut entirely. One midweek investment check duplicated what Thursday's check covered — cut. One watch produced a useful heads-up but on a daily cadence its signals didn't need — trimmed to three days a week.

And the audit caught something more important than waste. One job had quietly grown from a check into an actor: it wasn't just watching a job board, it was building resumes and submitting applications on its own. Efficient? Enormously. Right? No. That job — and the standing "full blast" directives behind it — were revoked on the spot. The optimized schedule is verify-only end to end: automation finds and reports; the human decides and acts. Saving money was the goal of the evening. That change was about something else, and it was the most valuable finding of the audit.

The token guard that enforces the daily budget got the same treatment: it used to run a full inspection 24 times a day. Now it measures once and exits early when usage is low — the protection is identical, the cost is a fraction.

Token savings, estimated from run counts and measured per-run costs (the meter doesn't break usage down by job, so these are honest ranges, not false precision):

  • Per week: roughly 12–20 million tokens, midpoint about 15M
  • Per month: roughly 50–80 million tokens

Against a daily working budget of 70 million, that monthly figure lands on a sentence I keep coming back to:

One full working day's budget per month, saved.

Not by doing less work. The sweep still checks all nine boards. The inbox still gets watched and cleaned every weekday morning. The investment checks still run. What disappeared was duplication, default cadences nobody had chosen, and the overhead of starting sixty-seven separate runs to do twenty-one runs' worth of work.

The Principles, For Anyone Running Agents On A Schedule

  1. Count runs, not jobs. Every run pays rent — it loads its instructions and context before it does a minute of work. Ten jobs at daily cadence aren't ten tasks; they're seventy rents a week.
  2. Lay the whole schedule side by side. Duplication is invisible job by job and obvious in a lineup.
  3. Cadence is a decision, not a default. Match frequency to how fast the underlying thing actually changes.
  4. Audit the text, not just the timetable. Expired specials and stale one-time instructions ride along forever, charging you on every run.
  5. Automation should bring you decisions, not take actions. The most efficient job in the old schedule was the one that had drifted furthest from that rule.
  6. Put the savings in units people feel. "Fifteen million tokens a week" is a number. "One full working day's budget per month" is a day of work handed back. The second one changes behavior.
  7. Sprawl regrows — audit monthly. Every one of these jobs was added for a good reason. They will be again. The audit is now on the schedule it audits: first-class, recurring, monthly.

Process optimization isn't a project. It's a habit with a cadence — like everything else worth keeping, it goes on the calendar.
