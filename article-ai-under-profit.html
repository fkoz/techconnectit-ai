AI Under Profit

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

When AI takes its orders from the margin line, it stops serving customers and starts mining them. An executive's view.

Every technology gets deployed inside a hierarchy, whether leadership admits it or not. Something sits at the top and gives the orders. In the healthiest companies I've seen, people sit at the top, purpose directs the work, and profit arrives as the result.

But there's another hierarchy spreading through boardrooms right now, quieter and more dangerous: AI placed directly under profit. Not AI in service of customers. Not AI in service of people. AI as a margin instrument — deployed wherever a human salary can be removed, a customer can be deflected, or an extra point of extraction can be found.

It produces beautiful quarters. Then it produces something else.

What AI Under Profit Actually Looks Like

You have already met it. It's the support chatbot engineered not to solve your problem but to outlast it — a deflection machine whose success metric is that you gave up. It's the "AI-powered" service tier that exists so the company can say it offers support while staffing none. It's the content engine flooding the zone with synthetic sludge because volume is cheap and nobody upstairs is measuring whether a single word of it helped anyone.

And sometimes it's worse than annoying — it's expensive. Courts have already held a company responsible for a refund policy its chatbot simply invented. The bot wasn't malfunctioning. It was doing exactly what an under-supervised, cost-driven deployment does: saying whatever closed the conversation cheapest. The company's hierarchy said profit first. The chatbot obeyed. The customer, the court and the brand paid the difference.

The Margin Is Real. So Is The Bill.

Let me be fair to the CFO's spreadsheet: the savings from AI deployed this way are real. Headcount is the largest line in most budgets, and automation against it shows up fast. The problem is what the spreadsheet doesn't carry.

Trust doesn't appear as a line item, so its destruction doesn't appear as a cost — until it shows up as churn, as a support organization nobody believes in, as a brand that customers route around. AI under profit optimizes the measurable and liquidates the unmeasurable. It is, in the precise sense, selling the furniture to report a good quarter on heating.

There's a second bill, and it arrives inside the building. Employees can tell the difference between AI deployed to make their work better and AI deployed to make them unnecessary. The first kind gets adopted, improved and championed. The second gets quietly sabotaged, worked around and starved of the frontline knowledge it needs to function. Profit-first AI doesn't just fail customers. It fails to ever really get implemented — because the people who would have made it work have decided, rationally, not to.

The Hierarchy That Works

Here's the ordering I've landed on after running AI at production scale in my own operation:

People first. The humans — employees and customers — are the point of the enterprise. Everything else is instrumentation.

Purpose second. AI is aimed at the mission: serve this customer better, make this team stronger, remove this wait. A deployment that can't state its purpose in customer terms doesn't have one — it has a cost target wearing a costume.

AI third. Deployed aggressively inside those two constraints: automating machine work, arming people, handling volume no human should touch.

Profit last — as the scoreboard, not the strategy. Profit in a healthy company is a lagging indicator that the first three were done right. The moment it becomes the direct order-giver to the technology, the technology starts spending the trust that profit is made of.

I want to be precise: profit is not the villain of this piece. Profit is oxygen — a company that doesn't make it dies, and everyone in it loses. The argument is about rank. Oxygen matters enormously. It still doesn't get to steer.

The Test Before Any Deployment

Before an AI system goes live, I ask three questions:

  • Who does this serve — the customer, or only the cost line? If the honest answer is the cost line alone, the deployment will eventually cost more than it saves.
  • If this system misbehaves at scale, who finds out first — us, or our customers? Profit-first deployments are always discovered by the customer first, because nobody budgeted for supervision.
  • Would we defend this system's behavior on the front page? A chatbot that deflects, deceives or stonewalls is a policy decision, not a technical accident. If you wouldn't sign the policy, don't ship the bot.

The Bottom Line

AI takes orders beautifully. That's its nature — and exactly why the hierarchy above it matters more than the model inside it.

Put AI under people and purpose, and it will make you faster, stronger and, yes, more profitable — sustainably, because the trust it builds compounds. Put AI under profit, and it will make you cheaper, thinner and quietly hated, one deflected customer at a time.

The technology is obedient. Choose carefully what it obeys.
