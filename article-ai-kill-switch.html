Design the Kill Switch Before You Need It

By Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT.AI · November 13, 2026

Every autonomous system I run has brakes. Not because I expect it to fail. Because I have spent a career in quality, and I know the difference between a system that can be stopped and a system that is merely being watched.

Watching is not control. Watching is how you get a front-row seat to the accident.

On October 30, 2026, my operation's kill switch fired. It was not a drill, and nobody panicked, because the stop had been designed months earlier, in a calm moment, by people who were not in the middle of a problem. That is the only time a kill switch ever gets designed well.

Here is what happened, and what it taught me.

The Day the Line Tripped

My AI operation runs on a daily token budget: 70 million tokens a day, measured continuously. Token usage is our spend, and spend is the honest meter of how much autonomous work is actually happening.

That evening I had parallel runs going — multiple agents working at once. Parallel work is fast, and it burns fast: at peak, those runs were consuming roughly 10 million tokens an hour. Between one check and the next, the day's usage crossed the tripwire just under the cap.

The system did not send me an alert and wait for a decision. It stopped. All automated work paused, on the spot, and stayed paused until the next morning's boundary. The runs in flight were halted mid-task. Nothing restarted itself.

People hear that story and call it a failure. It was the control working exactly as designed, at exactly the moment it was needed, without requiring me to be awake, attentive, and correct under time pressure.

Design It in the Calm

A kill switch has four parts, and all four have to exist before the bad day.

First, a measurable line. "Too much" is not a line. A budget number is. Ours is a daily cap with a tripwire just below it, so the stop fires with margin instead of after the damage.

Second, warnings that tighten as you approach the line. Routine checks when usage is low. Closer checks as the day climbs. Very close checks near the tripwire. The cadence is part of the control — a measurement you take once an hour cannot catch a fire that burns in fifteen minutes.

Third, the stop itself is automatic. No approval step, no human in the loop of the stopping. Human judgment is for the restart and the diagnosis. The stop belongs to the machine, because the machine is the only thing guaranteed to be watching at the speed the machine works.

Fourth, consequences with memory. An overrun is not forgiven at midnight. Whatever we burn over one day's cap is subtracted from the next day's cap. The system remembers being reckless, and the next day runs leaner because of it.

And one more rule came out of October 30, aimed at the cause rather than the symptom: concurrency is capped at one agent and one browser at a time. Parallel runs were the burn rate. A kill switch that treats the fire but not the fuel line is a control that will get exercised again.

Notice what the design does not include: an automatic restart. When the stop fired that evening, the schedule stayed down until the next morning — not because the system was sulking, but because resuming autonomous work after a trip is a diagnosis, not a reflex. Somebody reads the record, works out what burned and why, and then turns the line back on with the cause addressed. A stop that quietly resets itself at midnight teaches the operation that the line is decorative. Ours teaches it that the line is real.

Why Improvised Stops Arrive Late

Every operation that lacks a designed stop believes it has one. The belief sounds like this: "If something goes wrong, I'll notice and shut it down."

Run the timeline honestly. The system works at machine speed. You notice at human speed — after the unusual result, after the second look, after the meeting. Then you decide whether it's real. Then you figure out how to stop it, which, if you have never designed the stop, means finding the right screen, the right job, the right process, while the thing you are trying to stop is still running.

That is not a stop. That is a chase.

On a factory floor, nobody improvises the emergency stop. The cord is on the wall, painted red, reachable from where the work happens, and every operator knows that pulling it is never punished. We would call a plant manager reckless for running a line without one. Then we deploy software agents — systems that can send, spend, publish, and act — with nothing but a hope and a dashboard.

Brakes Are What Make Speed Possible

Here is the part the vendors leave out of the demo: the kill switch is not the opposite of autonomy. It is the license for it.

I run agents overnight and hand them real work precisely because I know the boundaries are mechanical. The budget stops the spend. The gates stop the sends. The pause authority stops everything else. Autonomy I cannot stop would not be autonomy I could afford to grant.

So before you scale an agent, answer four questions in writing. What is the line? Who or what watches it, and how often? Does the stop fire on its own, or does it wait for a person? And what does the system remember afterward, so the same fire costs less next time?

If any answer is "we'll figure it out," you do not have a kill switch. You have a plan to improvise one, on the worst day, at machine speed.

Design the brakes while the road is dry. You will need them exactly once, and once is enough.
