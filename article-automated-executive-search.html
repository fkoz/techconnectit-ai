The Automated Executive Search: 100+ Applications in Two Days — and What It Proves About AI Adoption

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

Frank Koziol is a senior technology leader across infrastructure, data & analytics, security, quality, and AI governance — most recently at Boeing, where he held senior leadership roles in analytics/AI and infrastructure and served on the enterprise AI governance team. People first. Technical excellence. All-in, all-win.

Every executive I know has an AI strategy. Very few have an AI operating model. That gap — between buying the tool and redesigning the work around it — is where most adoption quietly fails.

I know, because I just ran the experiment on myself.

Returning to the market after thirty-plus years in technology leadership, I found an executive search process that no CIO would tolerate inside their own organization: dozens of disconnected portals, no system of record, stale data presented as current, forty-five minutes of manual effort per transaction, and no reliable metrics anywhere. Candidates are expected to run this on spreadsheets and hope.

I run operations for a living, so I rebuilt it the way I would rebuild any broken enterprise process: define the operating model first, then let automation execute inside it. In a single two-day push, the system submitted 135 applications — each to a verified-live posting, each with a résumé tailored to that role's required qualifications. The running total is 173.

Here is the model, stated in the language executives actually manage in.

1. Master data before automation

Every failed automation program I have ever seen automated chaos and called it transformation. So the first artifact wasn't a tool — it was a governed record: complete work history, education, certifications, references, and pre-authorized answers to every recurring screening question, decided once and held as the single source of truth.

That is a data governance decision, not a clerical one. An AI executing against a verified record produces accurate work at scale. An AI improvising produces risk at scale.

2. Controls, not trust

Job aggregators routinely re-index postings that closed weeks earlier. My system therefore verifies every opening on the employer's official site before it enters the queue — a control, executed every time, because the cost of acting on bad data is wasted executive effort and a distorted pipeline.

The same discipline applies to the AI itself. It has, on occasion, handed me a wrong date with complete confidence — once surfacing a "verified" role that had closed the day before. In an enterprise, we would call that a model-risk event, and the response is never to abandon the system. It is to strengthen the control environment: verify sources, label data freshness, correct the record rather than the answer, and log the failure so the process — not just the output — improves.

3. Portfolio prioritization

The queue is ranked the way a CIO ranks a project portfolio: hard deadlines first, then value — and value, at this level, is defined honestly. A defined-benefit pension. Remote or hybrid flexibility. Equity participation. Level fit. The system flags roles below the target level rather than silently applying, because prioritization without decision rights is just a list.

4. Delegated authority — and decision latency

The largest hidden cost in any workflow is decision latency: work idling while it waits for a human to answer a question that was answered last week. I codified the recurring decisions as standing orders — the equivalent of a delegated authority matrix — and the system executes within them without re-asking.

The few steps that genuinely require the principal — a legal attestation, a verification, a credential only I hold — are not allowed to interrupt the flow one at a time. They are flagged, the operation moves on to unblocked work, and the gates are cleared in one short session. In enterprise terms: I removed myself as the bottleneck of my own process. Executives reviewing their organizations should ask how many workflows are quietly waiting on them in exactly the same way.

5. Manage to the front-end metric

One tracker is the system of record, and one number runs the operation: total applications submitted. Not activity, not drafts — output. In any transformation I have led, the discipline is identical: agree on the metric that represents real throughput, publish it daily, and let the scoreboard drive the improvement conversations. Sentiment follows data.

What governance looks like from the inside

I spent part of my Boeing tenure on an enterprise AI governance team — the cross-business-unit group working through AI regulation and standards. The frameworks we discussed at that altitude are sound, but they become real only at the operating level: human-in-the-loop where judgment is irreversible, full audit trail of what was submitted where, and a human accountable for the output. My search runs on exactly those principles. The AI drafts, verifies, queues, and executes inside its authority. Judgment — which roles are worth changing a life for, how to walk into an interview, how to build a relationship — stays with the executive. Before every interview, the system prepares my brief, including a role-specific answer to "why this role." I still have to walk in and mean it.

The lesson for executives

AI adoption does not fail because the models are weak. It fails because leaders automate tasks instead of redesigning operating models — no governed data, no control environment, no delegated authority, no metric. Run one process — even your own job search — under real operating discipline with AI inside it, and you will understand more about your organization's AI readiness in a week than a year of vendor briefings will teach you.

Process optimization is what I do for a living. It appears to work on a career, too.

Fellow executives: where is decision latency hiding in your organization — and which of your standing decisions should your team stop waiting on you for?
