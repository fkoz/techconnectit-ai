What My AI Actually Costs: A Token Audit Of My Own Operation

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

Everyone asks what AI costs. Almost nobody can show you the ledger. I opened mine.

For eighteen days I've run a serious operation on AI — a full-scale job search (155 applications and counting), an investment portfolio's research and monitoring, a family-history research program, and, lately, a small magazine's worth of articles. It all runs on a token budget: 500 million tokens a week, metered, the way a plant meters power.

So I did what I do with every operation I run: I audited it. Not the vendor's brochure numbers — my actual usage records, process by process, day by day. If you're spending real money on AI, in either direction, here's what the bill actually looks like from the inside.

[[CHART]]

The headline: two processes eat 96 cents of every token

Live browser work — 52.3% of everything. The single most expensive thing my AI does is operate websites: opening career portals, creating accounts, filling multi-page applications, uploading résumés, verifying the upload registered, recovering when a page crashes. A clean application is cheap. But browser work pays for every page three times — read it, act on it, re-read it to confirm — and a long session compounds. My most expensive single session cost 70 million tokens: roughly 1% of my entire eighteen days, in one sitting, on one employer's portal.

The standing conversation — 44.1%. The second whale isn't a task at all. It's this: the ongoing thread where the operation lives. Every message I send re-reads the accumulated context — the standing instructions, the day's state, the history we're building. Memory is not free; it's metered rent, and the rent rises with everything the operation remembers. The main thread alone is 28% of all my usage. My chief of staff's memory is my second-largest cost center, and I'd pay it again — but nobody should pretend it isn't on the bill.

The rework tax is real, and it's the part nobody budgets

Here's the finding that matters most to anyone who has ever run a factory floor: the costliest line items weren't heavy use. They were broken processes.

One application consumed 57 million tokens — not because the job was big, but because the portal's résumé parser shattered my work history into 25 duplicate entries that had to be consolidated by hand, and the account had to be created after the saved login was rejected. One employer's password-reset flow silently refused to send its email across seven attempts — every attempt billed, zero applications produced. One application platform crashed the browser four separate times; each restart re-billed the attempt from the top.

Rework is the most expensive work in any operation humans have ever run. It turns out that's still true when the worker is a machine. The tokens weren't wasted on AI being expensive. They were wasted the way money is always wasted: on defects, retries and processes that don't work the first time.

The cheap seats will surprise you

Everything executives imagine is expensive is nearly free:

  • All my background automation combined — the daily scans, market watches, deadline alerts, inbox monitors — is 2.7%. Breadth is cheap. A hundred quiet sentries cost less than one bad afternoon in a browser.
  • Documents and research are about 1%. Twenty magazine articles, dozens of tailored résumés, a full market research report — rounding error. Text in, text out, no page-state to churn.

And concentration is brutal: my three most expensive days account for 44% of all usage in eighteen days. AI cost isn't a utility curve. It's a spike chart, and the spikes have names — blitz days.

What the meter did to my management

Last week I used 100% of my allowance — hit the wall. This week I'm at 90% on Tuesday, with the reset not due until Friday. I don't tell you that for sympathy. I tell you because the meter changed how I run the operation, the way a P&L changes how you run a plant: work is ranked (job search first, investments second, the genealogy hobby capped at 40% by my own rule), gates get batched so nothing idles, postings get verified before the expensive application starts — a ghost job killed in thirteen steps costs almost nothing; a ghost discovered at step two hundred costs a fortune.

The truth about your AI bill

Most companies will never see an audit like this, and that's the real problem. Their AI cost hides inside flat subscriptions, spread across seats, unaudited and unexamined — which means their process cost hides too. Tokens did something no software pricing model has ever done for knowledge work: they itemized it. Every retry, every crashed page, every bloated workflow now has a number next to it.

Your AI bill is not the cost of AI. It's the cost of your processes — finally, mercifully, itemized.

Run the audit. The whale is never where the brochure said it would be.
