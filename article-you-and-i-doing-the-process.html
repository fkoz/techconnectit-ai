You and I Have Been Doing the Process

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

Field notes from a two-person operation — one human, one machine, and a workflow we rebuilt while it was running.

The other articles I've written started as management theory. This one didn't. This one is a field report.

For the past several weeks I've run a real operation — a full-scale executive job search, an investment watch, a family-history research program — with a team of two: me, and an AI agent. Not AI as a search box. AI as staff: researching, drafting, applying, tracking, remembering everything, and working while I was in meetings, at dinner, and asleep.

People ask what it's like. The honest answer: it's like managing the most capable and most literal employee you've ever had — one who never sleeps, never forgets, and occasionally needs you to redesign its job in the middle of the shift. Which is, of course, what managers are for.

The division of labor wrote itself

Nobody sat down and designed who does what. The work sorted itself, the way work always does, along a line anyone who has run operations will recognize.

The machine took the volume: more than 150 applications researched, verified live, tailored, and submitted. Board scans every morning. Verification codes fetched, forms filled, deadlines tracked, a ledger kept current. Everything rules-based, repetitive, and unforgiving of a missed detail went to the side of the team that never gets tired or bored.

I kept the judgment: which companies deserve a flag and which deserve another chance. When to override a screen and apply anyway. What my number is. The interviews. The relationships. Every decision that turns on who I am rather than what the data says.

Automate the mechanical, reserve the human for judgment, and never confuse the two. It works on a factory floor. It works here.

We optimized the process while it was running

Here's the part I didn't expect: the process itself became the project.

In the beginning, the agent brought me every snag — a login that failed, a code it needed, a form that wanted a decision. Sound familiar? It's how most organizations run. Every exception escalates, the executive becomes the bottleneck, and the work waits on the one person whose calendar is fullest. My own rule is to find where work waits and remove the wait. The wait was me.

So we redesigned the workflow live, mid-operation. Routine sign-ins: standardized, account-first, no escalation. Exceptions: batched into a single session instead of a dozen interruptions. Resets and codes: pulled automatically instead of requested. Updates: one report at the end instead of a drip of status. Dead ends: logged quietly and dropped.

Each redesign made the operation faster and my involvement smaller — which was the point. My job moved up a level: from doing the process, to designing it, to governing it. That's not an AI story. That's the oldest management story there is. The tool just compressed it into weeks instead of years.

What the human is actually for

Let me be precise about my remaining job description, because people get this wrong in both directions.

I set direction: which employers, which lanes, which risks, what "good" looks like. I make the calls that carry values — including flagging a company whose application process was so abusive it earned a permanent note, even after the application went in anyway. I am the accountable name on every submission. And I do the parts that are irreducibly personal: sitting across from a recruiter, being vouched for by colleagues I've worked beside for decades, and spending the hours the machine bought back on my family.

The machine is better at the process than I am. I am better at being the person the process serves. Neither fact threatens the other.

The takeaway for executives

You don't adopt AI by buying it. You adopt it the way you'd onboard any powerful, inexperienced, tireless team member: give it the volume work, watch its output, redesign its job as you learn, keep the judgment calls, and shrink your own involvement to direction, exceptions, and accountability.

Then decide — deliberately, because nobody budgets for this — what you'll do with the time it hands you. The time is the entire point. A well-run process gives hours back. Spend them on the human aspects: your people, your family, your judgment, your presence.

You and I have been doing the process — my agent and I, every day, at production scale. The process works.

The human still runs it.
