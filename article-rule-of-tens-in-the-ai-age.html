The Rule of Tens in the AI Age

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 30, 2026

Every business owner knows the most expensive sentence in business: "We'll fix it after it ships."

Quality management has a name for why that sentence is so expensive: the 1-10-100 rule — the rule of tens — credited to George Labovitz and Yu Sang Chang. A defect caught where it is made costs about 1 to fix. Caught at review, before it goes out the door, about 10. Caught after it reaches the customer, about 100 — rework, recovery, apology, and reputation, all billed at once.

We run an AI-first company: agent-run production processes doing real work on real schedules, with real money attached to the outcomes. From our own operation, with our own numbers: the rule of tens doesn't just survive the AI age. It gets sharper — because in an AI operation, the unit of production cost is the token, and tokens burn fast.

Why AI changes the stakes

A human team's defects propagate at the rate people can type, file, and ship. Bad — but bounded.

An AI operation produces at machine speed and machine volume. Our agents research, draft, build, check, and file around the clock — that speed is how a small company does the work of a much larger one. But defects scale at exactly the same speed. A flawed instruction, a bad data source, a missing review step: an unattended agent will execute that flaw faithfully, at volume, at 2 AM, while nobody is watching.

Every agent run spends tokens, and an unreviewed run can spend the entire "100" stage of the rule in minutes — budget consumed, defective work delivered, recovery still to pay for on top.

That's why, in an AI operation, quality stops being a department and becomes an architecture decision. You build the stages into the process itself — or you pay the hundred.

The three stages, mapped to tokens

Stage 1 — the plan. Before work starts, the token budget for the run is set, and templates are fixed at the source — standards live in the template, not in someone's memory. A defect prevented here costs almost nothing: a line in a plan, written before a single token is spent.

Stage 10 — the review gates. Before any heavy run, the meter gets checked. Work product passes an independent review, by a reviewer who didn't produce it, and a sample of output goes to a human for surveillance. Defects caught here cost roughly ten times the plan stage — and are still the cheapest defects you'll ever actually catch. Skip this stage to save the ten, and you're volunteering for the hundred.

Stage 100 — the escape. The budget is gone, the defective work is in a customer's hands, and you pay three times: the wasted spend, the fix, and the recovery. The only choice you ever had was whether to meet the defect at 1, at 10, or at 100.

What the rule looks like in our own shop

These are our own operating results, from the last few weeks.

The résumé gate

On October 30, 2026, an executive résumé built by one of our processes was headed to an employer. Before it went out, it passed a new standing rule: an independent reader — a reviewer who did not build the document — checks every résumé for completeness, the correct address for the role, and a genuine match against the posting's requirements.

The reader caught two truncated work-history date cells: two employers' date ranges, clipped in the document, invisible to the process that built it. Fix cost: minutes, at the review stage. A 10.

We know what the 100 looks like on this exact process, because our baseline has it. Before the gate existed, a defective submission went out and had to be locked — and another résumé carried the wrong city's address header, caught not by any process of ours but by the founder personally. The founder is the most expensive inspector any company has; his time at the end of the chain is the definition of the 100 stage.

So the gate is now one of three standing rules: independent review before anything goes out; one in five finished documents sent to the founder for surveillance sampling; and a two-strikes rule — a process that fails twice in a row stops and waits for a human decision instead of burning more tokens retrying its way out of a hole. An agent that keeps retrying a broken step isn't being diligent. It's spending the hundred in installments.

The token week

Our weekly allowance is 500 million tokens. The week of October 23–29, 2026 taught us what an ungated operation does to a meter.

One day's queue — job applications, research runs, website work, all legitimate, all authorized — burned roughly 90 million tokens and ran straight through a 10-million-token reserve we'd set aside, down to about 5 million remaining, before a human called the stop. The full 100-stage cost arrived on schedule: the wasted spend, 34 scheduled jobs disabled, in-flight work halted mid-task, and a recovery day restarting what should never have stopped.

The fix is staged the way the rule prescribes. Budget at plan time — that's the 1. A meter re-check before every heavy run, not just once in the morning — that's the 10. And a hard automatic pause when the reserve line is hit, so the operation stops itself instead of spending into the wall. Toyota's workers know that stop cord by its Japanese name. In an AI shop, it's a token threshold. Same idea, same discipline.

The gate found too late

On October 29, a website deployment stalled overnight. The work was fine; the plan was fine. What stopped it was a single human-only authentication step, discovered at the end of the chain, after a night of scheduled work had queued up behind it and stalled.

A human-only step surfaced at planning costs 1: you schedule around it. Found mid-run, it costs 10: you park the work and resequence. Found after a night of stalled production, it costs 100. Our rule now: every human-only step on the critical path gets surfaced at the first check-in of the day, screen already open. Gates are cheap at 9 AM. They're brutal at 9 AM the next day.

The unverified feed

One more, briefly. In October, an automated data feed in one of our financial workflows reported a position incorrectly, and an early draft flagged a problem that did not exist. Nothing was acted on — verification caught it — but the lesson stands: acting on unverified data is a 100-stage error purchased at full price. Our rule: automated feeds are indicative only; before anyone acts, the figure gets verified against the source. The feed proposes. The source disposes.

The numbers underneath

We count our waste the way a factory counts scrap. In our first 15 counted days, October 14–28, we logged 107 waste events: 46% defect fixes, 29% restarts, 25% rework — and roughly 27% of defects escaped downstream, to the expensive end of the rule.

Now every defect gets tagged with the stage where it was caught — 1, 10, or 100 — and we report the relative cost weekly against that baseline. The first résumé through the new gate avoided roughly 180 relative cost units on two defects alone, simply because they were met at 10 instead of 100.

The principle

The whole article in one line: in the AI age, the cheapest token is the one you never spend on a defect, and the second cheapest is the one spent catching it before your customer does.

Speed is not the hard part anymore; anybody can spend tokens. The hard part — what separates a production AI operation from an expensive experiment — is deciding, in advance and in writing, where your defects will be met: at the plan, at the gate, or in your customer's hands at a hundred times the price.

Our company's promise is "Proof, or you don't pay." That promise is only makeable if the proof is built into the process — staged budgets, independent review, sampling, hard stops — rather than inspected in afterward at 100-stage prices. Catch it at 1, gate it at 10, and never meet it at 100.

Always deliver more than promised. The rule of tens is how we make sure "more" never includes defects.
