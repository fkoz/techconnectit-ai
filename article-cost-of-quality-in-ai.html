The Cost of Quality in AI

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 29, 2026

By Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC

Phil Crosby wrote a book in 1979 called Quality Is Free. Most people remember the title and miss the argument: quality doesn't cost money — the lack of it does. You don't pay for doing the work right. You pay, heavily, for doing it wrong and then doing it again.

I run my company with an AI agent as my operations staff. Job applications, investment tracking, research, two websites — the agent does the work, I direct it. And because process optimization is my trade, I did to my own operation what I've done in every plant and program I've ever run: I counted the waste.

Since September 25, 2026, my daily reporting has logged every rework event, every restart, and every defect fix. Here is what bad quality costs, measured in my own shop.

The Count

Across the counted days, my operation logged 107 waste events:

  • 49 were defect fixes — 46%. Work that came out wrong and had to be repaired.
  • 31 were restarts — 29%. Sessions and tasks that died mid-flight and had to be started over.
  • 27 were rework — 25%. Work that was completed, then had to be completed again.

Read that the way a quality manager reads it: 54% of all my waste was doing work twice. Not exotic failure. Not bad luck. The same work, paid for two times.

In my world the currency is tokens — the unit my AI operation runs on, metered at 500 million a week. One job application, a single one, burned 56.8 million tokens before it landed, most of it rework: a repaired file parse, an account created the hard way, the same ground covered again and again. That's more than a tenth of a week's entire capacity spent applying for one job. Crosby called this the Price of Nonconformance. I call it the rework tax, and for a stretch in October it was the biggest line item in my business.

The Week It Peaked

The week of October 16–22 produced 61 of the 107 events — 57% of all the waste in five weeks, in one. One employer's portal killed five browser sessions in a row; eleven work-history entries got typed, lost, and typed again. Another application went out the door defective — a structured section left blank because the form fought back and the process skipped it instead of working around it. Under my own defect rule, that submission was locked, marked, and the company went on my do-not-apply list. One defect, three costs: the failed work, the lost opportunity, and the rule I had to build so it never happens again.

That week also taught me the difference between a defect and an escape. A defect is an error you catch. An escape is an error your customer catches — or worse, one that ships. I counted 13 escapes in 35 days, a 27% escape rate against the defects I caught and fixed. Thirteen sounds small until you price them:

  • A résumé submitted with the wrong home address on it — caught after submission, repaired with appeal emails.
  • A note sent to an outside party without my approval — one sentence misread, one send that should never have happened. That one cost trust, which doesn't come back at token prices.
  • A button on my own company's website that did nothing when you clicked it. I found that one myself, testing my own signup form like a customer. It had passed every internal check we'd run.
  • Recommendations that should never have been made — an investment write on an expiry date that doesn't exist, a strike price below my own cost basis. Both stopped before any money moved, but they reached the decision-maker. That's me. An escape doesn't have to leave the building to be expensive.

Internal failure costs tokens. External failure costs credibility. The old quality textbooks put it as the 1-10-100 rule: a defect costs a dollar to prevent, ten to catch in inspection, a hundred when the customer finds it. My numbers say the ratio is real — it's just denominated in trust at the far end.

What Prevention Bought

Here's the part the textbooks get right and most operations skip: the fixes are boring, cheap, and they work.

  • Verify before you submit. Every application now gets its review page checked line by line before it goes — born the day the defective one shipped.
  • Deduplicate before you start. One check against the master list killed a duplicate application that was minutes from going out. Prevention cost: seconds. Failure cost would have been a second application to the same employer.
  • Re-read blind. In my research work, a name in a century-old register only counts if it's verified by a second, blind read. My early reads misread names I was hunting for — because I was hunting for them.
  • Stop the line. We run a hard pause rule now: when the resource meter hits a floor I set, everything stops on its own — no "one more task." That's Jidoka, the andon cord, applied to an AI operation. The week I didn't have it, the meter ran dry.

And one more thing about the count itself: it's honest about its gaps. The counting started two weeks in, two days in the series never got counted, and I say so in the report. Quality data with the gaps labeled is worth more than a clean number somebody polished. The moment you punish the count, the count starts lying to you.

The Bottom Line

Crosby was right. Quality is free. What isn't free — what is ruinously expensive — is the rework: the form filled twice, the application sent wrong, the recommendation that never should have reached the desk, the week where 57% of five weeks' waste landed in seven days.

Run the count on your own operation. Not the output — the waste. Count what you did twice, what you fixed, and what your customer found before you did. Then spend your money on the boring end of that list, the prevention end, where a dollar still buys a hundred dollars of failure you never have to have.

That's the whole strategy. It was true on a factory floor in 1979. It's true in an AI operation in 2026. The tools changed. The arithmetic didn't.
