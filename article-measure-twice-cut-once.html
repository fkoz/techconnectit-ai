Measure Twice, Cut Once: Fixing the Ghosts in the Graveyard

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
November 3, 2026

Yesterday I wrote about the day my AI invented a woman named Catherine and carved her into my family tree — three confident readings of a gravestone that holds three names, not four. The hallucination was the defect. Today is about the corrective action.

In quality, we never stop at finding the defect. Finding it is the easy part. The discipline is in the fix: root cause, containment, corrective action, and then the part most people skip — making the fix stick so the next shift doesn't repeat the failure. "Measure twice, cut once" is the carpenter's version. In a program office, we called it verification.

Here is the uncomfortable truth about AI hallucinations: you cannot fix the model. The model is doing what it does — generating, completing, predicting. It will keep seeing things that aren't there. That is not a bug you patch. It is a characteristic you design around. The fix is never a better model. It is a better process around the model.

So here is the process. Five controls, built the way I built quality systems at Boeing — each one traceable to a real failure, each one cheap enough to run every time.

One: Measure twice. The AI reads the stone. Then it reads the stone again — and the second reading has to be independent, not a confirmation of the first. "Read me the entire stone top to bottom including dates" was my second measurement, and it failed, because the AI was confirming its own prior reading instead of re-reading the source. A real second measurement goes back to the raw material: the photo, the document, the primary record. Not the AI's summary of it. In manufacturing we called this independent verification — the inspector doesn't read the operator's notes, the inspector measures the part.

Two: Count the things. The simplest control is usually the strongest. "Three names and six dates" settled in five seconds what three confident readings could not. Every domain has countable things: names on a stone, line items on an invoice, fields on a form, requirements in a spec. Before you accept the AI's output, count what can be counted and compare. A mismatch between the count and the content is a defect flag, and it costs nothing to check.

Three: Never let the machine be the only witness. A fact the AI is the sole witness to is not a fact yet — it is a claim awaiting corroboration. In my tree, every name needs two witnesses or one primary record: the stone plus the census, the death certificate plus the obituary, the register act plus the index. Catherine had one witness — the AI — and the AI was wrong. This is the control that would have caught her on the first reading. If your workflow lets an AI-generated claim enter a system of record with no second source, you don't have a process. You have a hope.

Four: Make the correction cost something. When a defect escapes, the corrective action has to leave a mark on the system, not just on the conversation. I made my agent write the article about Catherine — the defect documented, the cause named, the example filed in its permanent instructions as the canonical case of hallucination-from-the-inside. In a quality system, that's the CAPA: corrective and preventive action, written down, assigned, and verified at the next audit. "I was seeing something that isn't there" is now standard work. The next shift — the next session, the next user — inherits the lesson without paying for the defect again.

Five: Calibrate confidence to evidence. The most dangerous part of the Catherine incident wasn't the error. It was the confidence. Three readings, three identical certainties, zero hedging. AI systems report confidence as fluency — the smoother the answer, the more certain it sounds, and fluency has no relationship to accuracy. So invert it: treat every confident AI output as unverified until the process says otherwise. Confidence is a property of the prose, not of the facts. The day I trust the tone instead of the verification is the day Catherine gets a headstone.

None of this is new. Measure twice, cut once. Trust but verify. Independent inspection. Two witnesses. Corrective action with teeth. These are old disciplines, and they work on AI for the same reason they worked on airframes: the failure mode changed, but the physics of verification didn't. You don't need a new philosophy for machine-generated errors. You need the old one, applied without embarrassment.

The carpenter measures twice because wood is expensive and mistakes are permanent. Data is cheaper than wood and mistakes feel reversible — until the wrong name is in the tree, the wrong number is in the report, the wrong fact is in front of a customer. Then you learn what the carpenter knew: the second measurement was always cheaper than the cut.

Three names. Six dates. No Catherine. Measure twice.

People first. Verify everything. In that order.
