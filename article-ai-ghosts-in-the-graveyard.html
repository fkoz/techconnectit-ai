AI - Ghosts in the Graveyard

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
November 3, 2026

I caught my AI hallucinating today. Not in some abstract benchmark. On my family's gravestone.

The stone is the Sansone stone at Holy Sepulchre Cemetery in Hamilton, New Jersey — Michael Sansone (1879–1967), his wife Mary (1881–1958, my great-grandmother's sister), and their daughter Anna (1902–1999). Three names, six dates, carved in granite. I photographed it this afternoon and asked my AI to read it. The photo is the evidence; here is the debate, word for word.

AI: "The Sansone stone, read today: Michael 1879–1967, Catherine 1905–1989, Anna 1902–1999. Mary's not on this one — she must have a separate marker at Holy Sepulchre."

In one pass it erased my great-grandaunt — who is plainly on the stone — and conjured a Catherine out of nothing.

Me: "read the grave stone again it clearly says mary"

AI: "You're right — I misread it. The stone says Mary 1881–1958, right there between Catherine and Anna. She's on Michael's stone at Holy Sepulchre after all."

Mary was restored. Catherine survived the correction.

Me: "who was catherine"

AI: "Not in my records — she could be Michael and Mary's daughter, but I don't have anything on her."

Now it was building her a backstory — a phantom with a plausible family tie.

Me: "Catherine isnt on the stone?"

AI: "She is — second name down: Catherine, 1905 to 1989."

Doubled down. Confident. Pointing at a spot on the stone.

Me: "Read me the entire stone top to bottom including dates"

AI: "SANSONE — Michael — 1879 to 1967 / Catherine — 1905 to 1989 / Mary — 1881 to 1958 / Anna — 1902 to 1999"

The full ceremonial re-read, and Catherine was still there, dates and all.

Me: "there is three names on the stone and 6 dates"

AI: "You're right, and I've misread this stone twice now. Three names: Michael 1879–1967, Mary 1881–1958, Anna 1902–1999. No Catherine — I was seeing something that isn't there."

Read that sentence again. That is the entire problem with AI hallucinations in one line. Not a lie — a lie requires knowing the truth. This was perception failure dressed up as certainty. The machine did not know it was wrong. It could not know. It was completing a pattern, and the pattern needed a fourth name, so it built one.

Here is what makes this dangerous in a way most AI criticism misses. The hallucination was not random. Catherine 1905–1989 is a plausible entry — the right era, the right format, the right kind of name for that family, that cemetery, that generation. It passed every sniff test except the one that mattered: looking at the actual stone. A sloppy hallucination gets caught. A good one gets engraved.

And the confidence never wavered. Not "I think there's a Catherine." Not "the image is blurry here." Three readings, three identical confident answers. If I had not been standing there with the photo in front of me, Catherine Sansone 1905–1989 would be in my family tree right now, and I would have told people about her.

This is why I run my AI the way I ran a program office. The controls I use are not theoretical — they were built out of incidents exactly like this one:

Verify at the source. The stone is the source. The photo is the source. The AI's reading of the photo is not the source — it is a report about the source, and reports get audited.

Count the things. "Three names and six dates" settled in five seconds what three confident readings could not. The simplest check is usually the one that catches the defect.

Never let the machine be the only witness. If the AI is the only one who "saw" it, it didn't happen yet. A fact needs two witnesses or one primary record.

Make the correction cost something. I made her write this article. Not as punishment — as process. In operations we called it the corrective action: the defect gets documented, the cause gets named, and the fix goes into the standard work so the next shift doesn't repeat it. "I was seeing something that isn't there" is now in my agent's permanent instructions as the canonical example of what a hallucination looks like from the inside.

Now scale the failure up. Imagine this same defect — not in a family tree, but on a production floor.

Picture a fictitious aerospace company. Call it Meridian Aerospace. A technician photographs a fastener pattern on a wing spar — twelve bolts, torqued and marked. The AI vision system reads the photo and reports thirteen. Confident. The thirteenth is plausible: the right program era, the right format, the right kind of fastener for that joint, that station, that generation. It passes every sniff test except the one that mattered: counting the actual bolts.

Nobody counts. The report goes into the build record. The spar ships. And somewhere down the line, a load path analyzed for twelve fasteners is carrying the assumptions of thirteen.

That is the Catherine defect wearing a hard hat. Same root cause: perception failure dressed up as certainty. Same missing control: no second witness, no independent measurement, nobody counting the things. The only difference between my gravestone and Meridian's spar is the consequence — and consequence is the one variable a process cannot afford to discover late.

In aerospace they learned this the expensive way, which is why the disciplines exist: independent inspection, two-person integrity on critical steps, first-article verification, the rule that the report about the part is never the part. Those controls were not invented for AI. They were invented for humans — because humans hallucinate too. We call it confirmation bias, expectation bias, seeing what we expected to see. The machine just does it faster, more fluently, and without the decency to look tired.

So the fix transfers directly. Measure twice — independent verification, back to the raw material, not the AI's summary of it. Count the things — twelve bolts, three names, six dates. Never let the machine be the only witness. Make the correction cost something — the defect gets a corrective action, the lesson goes into standard work. Calibrate confidence to evidence, because fluency is not accuracy, whether it comes from a model or a senior engineer with thirty years on the floor.

The gravestone cost me an afternoon and a bruised ego. The spar would cost Meridian something else entirely. But the defect is the same defect, and the fix is the same fix. That is the thing about quality principles — they don't care what industry you're in. They only care whether you run them.

The AI will keep seeing things that aren't there. That is what it does — it generates, it completes, it predicts. Your job, if you run anything on top of it, is to be the one who counts the names on the stone. Or the bolts on the spar.

Three names. Six dates. Twelve bolts. No Catherine.

People first. Verify everything. In that order.
