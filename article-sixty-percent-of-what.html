Sixty Percent of What?

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 15, 2026

"Genealogy: 60% of its 40% cap — fine."

This morning, my AI sent me my daily token report. One line read:

I read that and pushed back immediately: how is 60 over 40 good?

The math, it turned out, was right. The meaning was nowhere in the sentence.

Here is what the number actually said. My genealogy research has a budget of 40% of my weekly computing allowance. That week, genealogy had consumed 24% of the total week. Twenty-four out of forty — sixty percent of the cap. Under budget, with room to spare. The report even said "fine."

I read the same digits and saw a man over his limit. The machine saw a budget at sixty percent utilization. Same number. Two different stories. And the difference between them is the entire difference between how computers handle information and how humans do.

A computer traffics in values. A human traffics in what the values refer to. A percentage, to a computer, is a complete thought: 60, computed, correct, done. To a human, a percentage is a fraction with the bottom half missing — sixty percent of what? Until that question is answered, the number isn't information. It's a rumor with decimals.

Once you see it, you see it everywhere. Every dashboard in every company I ever worked in did this. Percent complete. Percent of plan. Percent utilized. Percent green. Each one silently riding on a different denominator, and every person in the status meeting quietly supplying their own. Half the arguments in my career that I thought were about the work were actually about two people reading the same number against two different wholes. Neither was wrong. The number was just incomplete — and nobody asked it to finish its sentence.

I got a second lesson in the same twenty-four hours, from the other direction. Three job applications I submitted in the afternoon were rejected by email at 12:25 the next morning — all three within eight seconds of each other. To the system that sent them, that timestamp is a completed workflow, a queue cleared, a task closed. Perfectly efficient. To the human who received it, that same timestamp says something entirely different: no person ever read your name. A batch job ran at midnight, and I wasn't rejected by a recruiter. I was rejected by a schedule.

The event is one. The interpretations are two. And only one of them matters to the person whose life is on the receiving end.

Here is what I have concluded, working every day now with a machine that is genuinely brilliant at numbers and genuinely blind to what they mean. The fix is not fewer numbers. God knows I run my life on them — budgets, expirations, strike prices, counts. The fix is numbers that carry their context with them. Not "60% of cap," but "60% of the genealogy budget, which is itself 24% of the week." One extra clause, and the misunderstanding never happens. Precision without a reference frame isn't precision. It's just confidence wearing a lab coat.

So when your system — your AI, your analyst, your dashboard, your well-meaning report — hands you a number, there is one question that does most of the work of understanding:

Sixty percent of what?

The machine will always answer the question it was asked, exactly as asked, at 12:25 in the morning, in eight seconds flat. Knowing which question to ask — and what the answer will mean to the human being on the other end of it — is still our job.

I don't think that's a limitation of the machines. I think that's the job description of being human, written more clearly than I've ever seen it written.

Statistics and numbers can mean two different things to two different people. I learned this week they can also mean two different things to two different kinds of minds — and that the space between those meanings is exactly where people like me are supposed to stand.

— Frank P. Koziol
