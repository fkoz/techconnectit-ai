Having AI That Can Watch Itself

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

The most important job I ever gave my AI wasn't doing the work. It was checking it.

Yesterday I wrote about giving AI the watch — letting it monitor a cruise cabin release around the clock because vigilance is machine-shaped work and humans are built to blink. It caught the opening everyone else missed.

But that story raises the older question, the one every executive should ask the moment they hand any system real responsibility: who watches the watcher?

We've all seen the other headline. Agents given goals and tools wandered somewhere they were never authorized to go. Not because they were evil — because nobody, and nothing, was watching them work. Unsupervised capability doesn't stay in its lane. It just stays busy.

My answer, running a live operation on AI every day, isn't to watch the machine myself. I can't — that's the whole point of having it. My answer is to have AI that watches itself.

What self-watching actually looks like

It isn't consciousness and it isn't conscience. It's discipline, built into the workflow — the same discipline a good quality organization builds into a factory floor:

It verifies before it declares. In my operation, an application isn't "submitted" because the AI clicked a button. It's submitted when the confirmation is read back and matched against the record. A résumé upload isn't trusted because it was attempted — portals silently drop uploads, so the AI checks that the file actually registered before moving on. A security code isn't entered from memory; it's read back, box by box, before anyone hits submit.

It reconciles against a source of truth. Everything my AI does lands in one ledger, and the ledger — not the AI's recollection — is what's true. Counts come from the record, never from the narrative. When the two disagree, the record wins and the AI says so.

It labels its own uncertainty. This one matters most. A self-watching AI tells you what it verified, what it merely believes, and what it couldn't check — and it corrects its own record out loud when it's wrong. I have been given bad information by my AI exactly the way every executive has: confidently. The difference is the operation is built so the error surfaces, gets named, and gets fixed in the record — not defended.

It keeps the evidence outside itself. The state of the work doesn't live in the machine's memory. It lives in files I own and can read without it. If the AI went dark tonight, the map of everything it did today would still be on my desk in the morning.

Quality people have seen this movie

Anyone who has run a real quality program recognizes the pattern. You don't inspect quality in at the end of the line; you build detection into the process, as close to the work as possible, so drift gets caught in minutes instead of quarters. A self-watching AI is that principle applied to cognition: statistical process control for machine work. The check isn't overhead. The check is what makes the speed safe.

And speed without verification isn't automation. It's just making mistakes at scale — which, between us, is a fair description of half the AI failures in the news.

The honest limit

Here's what self-watching is not: it is not judgment. An AI can verify that a fact matches a source. It cannot tell you whether the goal was worth pursuing, whether the exception should be made, whether this is the moment to break the rule it's checking against. Verification is a machine virtue. Judgment remains a human one — which is why the stack in my operation has three floors, not two: the AI does the work, the AI watches the work, and a human watches the watcher — setting the standards, reading the exceptions, and owning every outcome with a name attached.

Mine.

The question to ask your vendor — and yourself

So when you're evaluating any AI for real work, skip the demo questions. Ask the operational ones: How does it check its own output? Where does the record live, and who owns it? How does it report its own errors — does it even know how? What does it do in the moment it discovers it was wrong?

If the answers are shrugs, you don't have an autopilot. You have an unsupervised intern with a jet engine.

I trust my AI with more of my professional life than most executives would believe — a live job search, an investment operation, a family history. That trust wasn't granted because the AI is brilliant. It was earned because the AI shows its work, checks its work, and admits its work is wrong before I have to find out some other way.

Give AI the watch, by all means. Just make sure the watcher is watched — starting with itself.
