Is AI the Problem — or Is It Humans?

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

Every AI failure I've ever seen had a human upstream of it. An executive's view.

It's the argument of the moment. The AI gave a wrong answer. The AI project failed. The AI produced confident nonsense, exposed the company, disappointed the board. Case closed: AI is the problem.

I've spent the last several weeks running my professional life through an AI agent at production scale — hundreds of tasks, real stakes, real money and real career outcomes riding on it. So let me offer the view from inside the experiment:

In every failure I encountered, I can trace the fault to a human decision made before the AI ever ran. Every time. The AI didn't create the problems. It inherited them — and then, being a machine, it executed them at machine speed, in plain sight, where nobody could pretend anymore.

AI isn't the disease. AI is the diagnostic.

Exhibit A: the data

When AI produces garbage, the first suspect is never the model. It's the data — and data is a human artifact. Every wrong field was typed, approved, migrated, or never validated by people. A model trained or fed on your data is drinking from a well your organization dug. If the well is poisoned, don't blame the cup.

I wrote a whole piece on this: you cannot build an event-driven, data-driven organization on bad data. The AI version of that law is shorter: AI industrializes whatever it touches — including your mistakes.

Exhibit B: the process

The funniest thing about my own experiment? In the early days, the agent kept stopping to ask me things — logins, codes, small decisions — and the operation crawled. Leadership instinct says blame the tool. The truth was that the workflow was badly designed, and I was the bottleneck: every exception escalated to the one person with the fullest calendar.

The AI didn't change. I redesigned the process — standardized the routine, batched the exceptions, automated the retrievals, reported once at the end. Same agent. Transformed output.

Multiply that across an enterprise and you'll recognize the pattern behind most "AI failures": a broken process, automated faithfully. AI doesn't fix a bad workflow. It gives a bad workflow a bigger engine.

Exhibit C: the expectations

Humans sold this technology — and humans bought the brochure. Demos are choreographed; production is not. When a board expects the demo and gets production, the gap gets recorded as an AI failure. It was an expectations failure, set by people, in a sales process run by people.

The same goes for fear. Half the resistance to AI in organizations has nothing to do with the technology's limits. It's humans protecting turf, headcount, and the familiar. Those are legitimate human concerns — but they're human concerns. The tool is just where they surface.

The honest counterweight

Let me not oversell. AI has real limits, and executives should know them cold. It can state falsehoods with total confidence. It has no judgment, no context it wasn't given, no skin in the game, and — this one matters most — no accountability. An AI cannot own an outcome. It cannot be fired, sued, shamed, or trusted. It can only be used.

But a limit is a specification, not a villain. A crane that can't lift ten tons isn't "the problem" when a load drops; the problem is the plan that asked it to. When a decision-maker ships an AI's unverified output into a real decision, there was a human at the switch. There is always a human at the switch. "The AI decided" is a sentence a human says to transfer blame to something that cannot object.

That's the actual risk in this technology — not that it errs, but that it offers organizations a place to park responsibility. Ownerless decisions are the failure mode. The fix is human: every consequential output gets a named human owner, or it doesn't ship.

The mirror

Here's what I've concluded from running this at scale: AI is a mirror with an engine attached. It reflects your data quality, your process discipline, your culture of ownership — and then it amplifies all of it. Strong organizations are getting stronger with it, faster. Dysfunctional ones are getting a high-resolution view of their dysfunction, also faster.

Which is why the same tool produces a triumph in one company and a write-off in the next. The variable was never the model. The variable is the organization holding it.

Humans wrote the data. Humans designed the process. Humans set the expectations, hold the accountability, and make the final call. Humans are the problem — the specific, fixable, ownable problem. And that should be the most hopeful sentence in this entire debate, because humans are also the only ones who can fix it.

Blaming AI for your organization's failures is like blaming the mirror for what you see in it.

Fix the face, not the mirror.
