The AI RIF Wave Is Coming

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

First the water recedes. Then the wave. An executive's view on what's actually about to happen to headcount.

Every tsunami starts the same way: the water pulls back from the beach, quiet and strange, before the wave arrives.

Corporate America is in the pullback right now. The hiring freezes in AI-exposed roles. The roles eliminated by "attrition" and never backfilled. The teams told to do the same work with fewer requisitions because "the tools should cover it." No announcements, no headlines. Just the water going out.

The wave — formal, announced, AI-justified reductions in force — is behind it. Here's why I believe it's coming, what it will actually look like, and what separates the executives who surf it from the ones it buries.

Why the wave is inevitable

Start with the math no CFO can unsee. The cost of AI capability falls every quarter. The cost of labor rises every year. A board that watches a competitor cut 15% of a function's cost with automation will not accept "we preferred not to" from its own management. Once one credible company in a sector moves, the rest follow — not because the technology forces them, but because competition does. That's how every labor wave in my lifetime has worked, from offshoring to cloud migration. The economics arrive first. The org charts follow.

And the sequence is predictable. Waves hit the rules-based, high-volume work first: tier-one support, content production, routine analysis, document processing, junior development tasks. Coordination layers go next — the roles that mostly move information between other roles. The wave climbs the ladder slowly, but it climbs.

The honesty problem: AI-washing

Now the part the press releases won't say. A large share of the "AI layoffs" already announced aren't really AI layoffs. They're corrections — pandemic overhiring, margin pressure, activist investors, ordinary restructuring — wearing AI as the acceptable costume. Blaming AI is easier than admitting you overhired, and it makes the stock go up instead of down. I've written before that in most AI failures, the fault sits with a human upstream. The RIF version is the same story: humans made the decision; AI makes the headline.

This matters because executives planning against a fake signal will plan wrong. If you cut because a competitor's press release scared you, you've outsourced your workforce strategy to their PR department.

What the wave will punish

The companies that get hurt won't be the ones that cut. They'll be the ones that cut by org chart instead of by work.

Cut by spreadsheet and you remove the exception-handlers: the people who know why the process exists, which customer needs the call instead of the email, where the bodies are buried in the system you just automated. AI handles the routine superbly — and the routine was never where companies lived or died. They live and die on the exceptions, and exception-handling is a human skill built over years. RIF it away and the automation runs beautifully until the first unusual Tuesday, when it runs beautifully off a cliff.

The wave will also punish the pipeline. Junior roles are the most automatable, so they're the first to go — and in ten years those companies will ask where the seniors went. You cannot hire a decade of judgment off the street. A company that automates its entry level has eaten its seed corn and called it efficiency.

What the smart executives are doing instead

  • Redesign the work before reducing the workforce. Automate tasks first, measure what's actually left, then size the team to the redesigned work. Most organizations that RIF first discover the real number afterward — and do it twice.
  • Use attrition before announcements. The pullback is a tool: freeze, redeploy, let natural exits do quietly what a RIF does brutally.
  • Protect the exception-handlers and the pipeline on purpose. Name the roles that carry judgment and apprenticeship, and take them off the spreadsheet before the exercise starts.
  • Say what's true. If the cut is about costs, say costs. Employees can survive honesty. What they never forgive is finding out the AI story was cover — and they always find out.

The individual's version

Everything above has a personal mirror. The defense against the wave isn't hoping your role is safe; it's moving up the judgment stack — owning outcomes instead of tasks, becoming the person the automation reports to, the one who handles what the system can't. I know this works because it's my own play, run in public: I didn't wait to be automated. I picked up the tool and automated my own workload first.

The choice

The wave is coming whether we name it or not. The only real choice executives get is the sequence: redesign first, then resize — or resize first and discover, in public, what the people you cut were actually doing.

One sequence keeps the company's capability, culture, and credibility intact. The other produces a great quarter and a hollow company.

The water is already going out. Surf, or get hit standing still.
