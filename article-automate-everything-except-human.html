I Can Automate Everything in My Life Except the Human Aspects

Frank Koziol, Founder & President, Tech ConnectIT, LLC
October 13, 2026

What a machine taught me about the work only people can do. An executive's view.

I have spent the last few weeks running an experiment on my own life. Job applications submitted by automation — over 150 of them, each one researched, verified, and tailored. Inboxes triaged. Markets monitored. Records, schedules, and research handled end to end.

It works. That's the first thing to say. The mechanical parts of a modern professional life — the forms, the portals, the follow-ups, the tracking — can be automated to a degree that would have sounded like science fiction five years ago.

But the experiment taught me something I didn't expect: the more of my life I automate, the clearer it becomes what automation is actually for. It's for everything except the parts that matter most.

What the machine does beautifully

Let's give it its due. Automation is tireless at the repetitive: entering the same facts into the fortieth portal, watching a job board for the one new posting that fits, checking prices at the same time every morning, remembering everything. It doesn't get bored. It doesn't forget. It doesn't have a bad Tuesday.

Every process improvement of my career has come down to finding where work waits and removing the wait — and automation removes waits at a scale no team of humans can match. If a task is rules, repetition, and volume, a machine should be doing it. Full stop.

What it cannot do

But here's the other column of the ledger.

A machine cannot sit across from a hiring manager and earn trust. It cannot hear the pause in a conversation and understand what wasn't said. It cannot mentor a young engineer through the worst week of her project, or stand in front of a team and take responsibility when the plan fails. It cannot exercise judgment when the data is incomplete and the decision is due anyway — which, in my experience, describes most of the decisions that actually matter.

It cannot be vouched for. When a former colleague puts his name next to yours as a reference, that's twenty years of shared work speaking. No system generates that. It's earned in hallways and hard weeks, one kept promise at a time.

And it cannot care. A few years ago I left a job I loved to help care for my mother after my father died. No automation does that. No workflow grieves, or sits with a parent, or shows up for family. The most important work of that season of my life had no dashboard, no metric, and no possible substitute.

The paradox

Here's what surprised me: automation didn't make the human parts smaller. It made them bigger.

When the mechanical work disappears, what remains is pure human work — judgment, relationships, presence, accountability. And that work becomes the differentiator, because soon everyone will have the same machines. The executive who wins won't be the one with the best automation. It'll be the one who spent the time automation bought doing the human work better: knowing his people, earning trust, making the call.

My automated job search can get me to a hundred doors. It cannot walk through a single one of them. An interview is one human being deciding whether to trust another. That's not a limitation of the technology. That's the technology telling us what it was for all along.

The executive takeaway

Automate aggressively — and aim the savings at people.

  • Automate the process, not the person. If a human being is doing machine work, that's not dedication. That's a design failure.
  • Spend the dividend on presence. Every hour automation hands back is an hour available for mentoring, listening, and being in the room. Leaders who reinvest that time in their people will outrun leaders who simply bank it.
  • Keep a human accountable. A machine can execute a decision. It cannot own one. The name on the outcome is still yours.
  • Protect the un-automatable. Trust, empathy, courage, care — treat them as core capabilities. In an automated world, they're the scarce ones.

My twin pillars have always been "people first" and "technical excellence" — in that order. This experiment didn't change the order. It explained it.

Automate everything you can. Then go be human at full strength. That's not what's left over after automation.

That's the point.
